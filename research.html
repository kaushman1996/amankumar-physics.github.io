Research

Research Overview

My research focuses on emergent phenomena in strongly correlated quantum many‑body systems, where long‑range interactions, geometric frustration, and quantum fluctuations combine to produce unconventional electronic order and transport. A recurring theme of my work is to understand how these effects qualitatively reshape finite‑temperature phase diagrams and response functions, often beyond the scope of conventional quasiparticle descriptions. I aim to develop controlled, microscopic connections between interacting lattice Hamiltonians and experimentally accessible observables such as electrical conductivity, thermal Hall response, and the thermal melting of correlated electronic phases.

My approach is primarily numerical, combining large‑scale exact diagonalization (including FEAST‑based methods), finite‑temperature Lanczos techniques, tensor‑network approaches, and Monte Carlo simulations. These methods allow access to strongly interacting regimes where perturbative or mean‑field approaches fail, while retaining a transparent link to microscopic degrees of freedom. Wherever possible, numerical results are guided by symmetry considerations and insights from many‑body field theory.

Generalized Wigner Crystals in Triangular Moiré Systems

A major component of my research concerns generalized Wigner crystal (GWC) phases in triangular moiré superlattices, particularly transition‑metal dichalcogenide heterobilayers. In these systems, long‑range Coulomb interactions stabilize charge‑ordered states at fractional fillings commensurate with the moiré lattice. While experiments have directly imaged such crystalline states and measured their melting temperatures, theoretical understanding has often been limited to zero temperature or simplified short‑range interaction models.

I carried out the first systematic finite‑temperature quantum studies of GWCs using extended Hubbard models with realistic interaction profiles. By comparing truncated short‑range models with long‑range Coulomb interactions, I established when simplified descriptions succeed and when they fail. This analysis explains experimentally observed asymmetries between different fractional fillings and clarifies the role of experimentally tunable parameters such as the gate‑to‑sample separation in controlling the stability of charge order.

Beyond classical Wigner crystals, I identified partially quantum‑melted “pinball” phases, in which a subset of electrons remains localized while the remaining electrons retain substantial mobility. These phases have no classical analogue and naturally account for metallic transport coexisting with strong charge order, as reported in recent experiments.

Quantum Thermal Melting and Entropic Effects

Classical approaches are known to significantly overestimate the melting temperatures of generalized Wigner crystals relative to experiment. To resolve this discrepancy, I studied the impact of quantum effects on thermal phase transitions across a range of fractional fillings. Using finite‑temperature Lanczos methods and classical Monte Carlo simulations augmented with perturbative quantum corrections, I mapped out temperature–interaction phase diagrams beyond the classical limit.

A central result of this work is the demonstration that, for certain fillings where the thermal transition is first order, quantum fluctuations can increase the melting temperature even as the corresponding order parameter is softened. This counterintuitive behavior can be understood by explicitly evaluating quantum corrections to both the internal energy and entropy of the competing phases. By analyzing the resulting free‑energy balance, I identified general conditions under which quantum effects stabilize ordered phases at elevated temperatures. These ideas provide a framework applicable beyond moiré systems to interacting electronic crystals more broadly.

Thermal Hall Transport in Kitaev Magnets

Another major direction of my research addresses thermal transport in frustrated quantum magnets, particularly Kitaev and Kitaev–Heisenberg models. Experiments on materials such as α‑RuCl3 have reported large thermal Hall signals, raising questions about the persistence of fractionalized excitations in magnetically ordered regimes.

Standard Kubo‑formula approaches to thermal Hall conductivity are difficult to implement numerically due to boundary contributions and the need to subtract energy magnetization currents. To overcome these challenges, I developed tensor‑network‑based methods that compute the thermal Hall response directly from edge currents in finite‑temperature purified states. This approach avoids assumptions about the underlying quasiparticle content and allows direct access to transport properties in interacting lattice systems.

Using these methods, I reproduced half‑quantized thermal Hall plateaus in the Kitaev limit at low fields and demonstrated the persistence of large, generally non‑quantized thermal Hall responses in the presence of additional interactions and magnetic order. These results provide a unified microscopic interpretation consistent with experimental trends in candidate Kitaev materials.

Quasiparticle Stability and Hilbert‑Space Localization

Quasiparticles provide a powerful organizing principle in weakly interacting systems, but their fate in strongly correlated regimes remains an open problem. I approached this question by recasting quasiparticle stability as a localization problem in the many‑body Hilbert space, where stable quasiparticles occupy a vanishing fraction of the spectrum as system size increases.

Using FEAST‑based exact diagonalization, I accessed large numbers of many‑body excited states in targeted energy windows, enabling the study of quasiparticle lifetimes beyond perturbative regimes. Applying this framework to Kitaev–Heisenberg and disordered t–J models, I demonstrated that fractionalized excitations can remain well defined deep into magnetically ordered phases, while conventional magnons and Landau quasiparticles undergo nontrivial metamorphoses. This perspective places conventional and fractionalized excitations on equal footing and sheds light on anomalous transport phenomena observed in correlated materials.

Ongoing and Future Directions

Ongoing projects extend these ideas to metal–insulator transitions at half filling in extended Hubbard models on triangular lattices, strange‑metal transport in doped Mott insulators, and two‑dimensional tensor‑network approaches to thermal Hall transport using PEPS. Across these directions, my goal is to develop quantitatively reliable diagnostics of emergent order and transport that can be directly confronted with experiments in quantum materials.
